## Key Takeaway

**A shop can be opened, but the pitfalls are hidden — proceed with caution.**

The author spent 5 years working directly and indirectly in the coffee industry. In the second half of 2024, they opened a coffee shop. On January 27, 2025 (just before Chinese New Year), they transferred the business out. It lasted nearly 10 months — here's a painfully honest summary of the experience.

---

## Background

- Always believed in strict budget control — use the least cost to make the business work
- Marketing was a weak spot; cautious about investing in small independent shops
- Came across a brand's co-branding partnership: financial investors cover upfront costs, the brand provides equipment, and equipment costs are deducted as a percentage of revenue
- After site inspection, foot-traffic analysis, and simulations — concluded "I'll definitely make money doing this myself"
- **Reality check**: the coffee era without delivery platforms is over. The true cost of delivery platforms was drastically underestimated.

## The Barriers to Opening a Coffee Shop

1. **Platform operations**: unfamiliar with promotion tactics across different platforms; trial-and-error wasted time and money; new accounts face a grace period for algorithmic traffic boosting
2. **Product R&D**: inventory risk; turnover rate impacts cash flow
3. **Customer acquisition cost**: online acquisition costs far exceeded expectations
4. **Brand selection**: a centrally-managed brand team is good in theory, but in practice the acquisition cost was underestimated

## Brand Partnership Cost Breakdown

Three parts: fixed costs, variable expenses, brand royalty deductions.

Special circumstances of this shop:
- A financial investor covered renovation, rent, property fees, deposits, etc.
- The brand provided equipment, deducting costs first from revenue share
- The author only paid for inventory deposits

## Hidden "Traps" of Brand Partnership

1. **High packaging and material costs**: materials accounted for 57% of total costs (procurement cost, not actual consumption) — extremely high for delivery-focused business (material costs had a near 1:1 relationship with revenue)
2. **Delivery platform fees**:
   - Cost per thousand impressions: max ¥1.7, min ¥1.4
   - Online gross margin was lower than offline; heavy discounts meant low actual payout
   - Gross margin ranged from 54% in summer to 47% in winter
3. **Warehousing and shipping fees**: even when sourcing from the brand, there were shipping and storage costs — adding over ¥2,000 per month
4. **Tax surcharges**: invoicing required additional tax payments, around ¥500 per month

## Operating Data

- Delivery-dominant model, with delivery accounting for over 85% of orders
- Material costs had a near 1:1 relationship with revenue
- Blended gross margin: 54% – 47% (summer to winter)
- Total hard costs over six months: ¥23,000

## Lessons Learned

1. Don't enter unfamiliar territory to do things you're not good at
2. Partnerships for small businesses are not recommended — unless it's a husband-and-wife shop
3. If you genuinely want to make money or maintain a decent blended gross margin, don't let delivery orders dominate
4. High delivery proportion + low average order value (< ¥25) → blended margin will always be below expectations
5. The real keys: brand power, supply chain, and operational capability — all together

## Final Thoughts

Traffic anxiety plagues every industry. Delivery platforms have become a place to "hunt for bargains" — merchants get traffic, but the profits are nothing to write home about. Given the razor-thin margins, the daily grind hardly feels worth it. But without delivery platforms, foot traffic isn't enough to cover costs either — merchants are caught between a rock and a hard place.
